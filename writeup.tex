\section{The Project (The Problem and the Four Bots)}

The goal is to get the bot to the fire-suppression button on a randomly generated ship before it burns. The ship is a square grid, and the bot can move to an adjacent open cell or stay put. Fire spreads every turn: a cell catches fire with probability $1 - (1-q)^K$, where $K$ is the number of burning neighbors. Following the TA clarification, the button itself can't catch fire, but its neighbors can burn and cut the bot off from it.

Each turn the bot picks a move and makes it. If it walks into fire, it fails. If it reaches the button, it wins right away, so the last step doesn't need to survive another fire update. Otherwise the fire spreads, and the bot fails if its cell ignites.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bot 1} plans one shortest path at the start (avoiding the initial fire) and just follows it.
  \item \textbf{Bot 2} replans the shortest path every turn, avoiding the current fire.
  \item \textbf{Bot 3} replans every turn and tries to avoid both the fire and every cell next to it (not counting the button). If no such path exists, it falls back to Bot 2's rule.
  \item \textbf{Bot 4} is our own design, explained next.
\end{itemize}

\section{Bot 4 Design}

All of the shortest-path bots share a problem: a cell that's safe right now can be on fire by the time the bot gets there. So with Bot 4 we wanted to pick the path with the lowest predicted fire risk at the moment we'd actually be standing in each cell. Unlike Bot 3's wider buffer, this uses $q$, how fire moves through the hallways, and when we'd arrive at each cell.

\textbf{Forecasting the fire:} Let $P_t(c)$ be our estimate of the probability that cell $c$ is burning after $t$ more fire updates. Burning cells start at 1 and everything else starts at 0. For a cell that can ignite, we update
\[
P_{t+1}(c) = P_t(c) + \bigl(1 - P_t(c)\bigr)\left[1 - \prod_{n \in N(c)} \bigl(1 - q\,P_t(n)\bigr)\right]
\]
Walls and the button can't ignite. The product estimates the chance that none of the neighbors light the cell, and we treat the neighbors as independent. That isn't exactly true, since nearby fires are correlated, but it's a cheap way to forecast without simulating tons of fires every turn. We only use it to rank routes, not as an exact survival probability.

If we'd be in cell $c$ after move $t$, the cost of that cell is
\[
w_t(c) = -\log\bigl(1 - P_t(c)\bigr).
\]
Maximizing a product of survival probabilities is the same as minimizing a sum of negative logs, so this lets us add up risk along a path. Safe cells cost close to nothing, and cells that are almost certainly burning cost a huge amount (walls and cells with $P_t \ge 1 - 10^{-12}$ get infinite cost). The button costs zero because we win as soon as we reach it. There's no separate weight for distance, so the bot will take a longer route whenever its total risk is lower, even by a tiny amount. When two arrivals tie, we take the earlier one.

\textbf{Searching through time:} First we compute the current shortest path that avoids fire, with length $L$ moves. Our planning horizon is $H = L + 20$, so we have some room to detour without the search blowing up. The 20 is just a number we picked, not something we tuned. Let $C_t(c)$ be the lowest total risk cost for getting to $c$ after $t$ moves. The bot's own cell starts at 0 and everything else at infinity, and each layer is
\[
C_t(c) = w_t(c) + \min_{n \in N(c)} C_{t-1}(n).
\]
Bot 4 picks the lowest-cost button arrival over moves $1$ through $H$, backtracks to find the first move, and makes only that move. Next turn we redo the whole forecast from the actual fire, so we never get stuck following a prediction that turned out wrong. The search lets us revisit cells but doesn't explicitly consider staying.

Two special cases: if the fire has already cut the bot off from the button, Bot 4 stays, since fire never goes away. If a safe path exists right now but every forecast route has infinite cost, the forecast is probably just too pessimistic, so we take the first step of the plain shortest path.

\textbf{Efficiency:} We use numpy shifts to compute the four-neighbor forecast and the best-predecessor step over the whole grid at once, and we cache the Boolean grid between turns. Each decision takes $O(HD^2)$ time and $O(HD^2)$ memory for the stored layers, so the horizon controls both how the bot behaves and how much computation it uses. Early benchmarks showed 80$\times$80 ships were doable but Bot 4 was a lot slower than the others. Those benchmarks were before the fireproof-button fix, so we don't treat them as current timings. For the new experiments we used 60$\times$60 ships.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\textbf{Setup:} For the full sweep we generated 100 ships of size 60$\times$60 for each $q \in \{0.0, 0.1, \dots, 1.0\}$. In each scenario we randomly placed the bot, button, and initial fire on three different open cells, then ran all four bots, for 4,400 runs total. All four bots got the same ship, starting positions, and fire seed, so they all saw the same fire. Since bot movement doesn't change how the fire spreads, this makes the comparison fair. We reused the same 100 scenarios across $q$ values.

We also ran a focused follow-up at $q = 0.3$ with 200 new scenarios, which together with the 100 from the sweep gives 300 per bot. In total that's 5,200 distinct runs. The sweep used seeds 600--609 and the follow-up used seeds 700--709, and the raw data stores the seeds, positions, and a 5,000-turn cutoff (no run ever hit it). The sweep took 827.3 seconds, and the whole collection took 999.9 seconds.

\section{What surprised us}

We expected Bot 4 to help most when the fire spreads fast enough to matter but slowly enough that detours are still useful. The graph matches that, since nearly every bot wins at low $q$ and the curves bunch up at high $q$.

At first we were worried the bots were basically the same. An early version of our demo had all four bots burn on the same first turn, and a small 10$\times$10 test gave overlapping curves. Controlled layouts and full-path replays showed they do make different decisions: Bot 3 takes buffer-driven detours, and Bot 4 sometimes picks a different move from Bots 2 and 3 with identical fire. If we were to just look at success rates, we wouldn't have seen those differences, especially when different choices end in the same result. 

The TA's clarification that the button can't burn also changed our process. Our first experiments let the button burn, so we separated those out and recollected everything. None of the old numbers are mixed in with the results here.

The biggest surprise was how small Bot 4's edge over Bot 3 is. A more expensive strategy isn't automatically much better. Trial 249 shows the downside, since a low-risk detour can lose a game that the direct route wins, and Trial 50 shows the upside. This shows us that a good bot has to balance risk against urgency, instead of assuming that more lookahead is always better.

\section{Ideal Bot and Computation Time}

An ideal bot would use everything it can see: the grid, its position, the button, the burning cells, and $q$. It would consider every action, including staying, and plan over all the ways the fire could play out, adapting its later moves to what it sees. That's different from committing to a single forecast route. The problem is that exact planning is huge, because any subset of open cells could be burning.

A more practical version would simulate a bunch of random fire futures for each candidate move and score each one with a fast escape policy. That would capture the fire correlations that our forecast ignores, though it costs more time and adds sampling noise. We didn't implement it.

In our simulator the fire waits for the bot to finish deciding, so slow computation never directly causes extra spread, and our timings don't show a real-time penalty. In a real system the bot would need a deadline: compute a quick fallback move first, then improve it if there's time. When fire is right next to the bot, a fast move matters more than a perfect plan that arrives late. When the fire is far away, extra computation is worth it.

The hint about guaranteed wins also suggests a way to collect data faster. Fire can only spread one edge per update, so if a route reaches every cell strictly before the earliest possible ignition and then reaches the button, it's safe even under the fastest possible spread. This certifies a specific route without running randomized trials, but it doesn't certify every route a bot might pick. We didn't use it in our experiments.
